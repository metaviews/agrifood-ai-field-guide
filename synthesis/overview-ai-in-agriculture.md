This is the overview of the whole field guide: a plain-language assessment of
what the research, taken together, says about artificial intelligence in
agrifood systems. It synthesises the syntheses — the cross-cycle
consolidations and the cross-case patterns listed below — into one picture.
It introduces no new claims; every assessment here is carried from the
underlying syntheses and units, which carry the named sources. Read this
first for the shape of the field, then follow the links for the evidence.

## The short version

AI in agriculture is real, but it is not arriving the way the headlines
suggest. Three things are true at once:

- **Deployment is narrow and unglamorous.** The AI that farmers actually
  bought and use is concentrated in a few practical tasks — spotting weeds
  and spraying only them, retrofitting existing machines to drive themselves
  on simple jobs, and sensing grain quality on the combine. The most-hyped
  applications — fully autonomous tractors working whole fields, swarms of
  small robots, and global "foundation models" that map every crop on Earth —
  are still mostly announcement, with capital and narrative far ahead of
  disclosed deployment.
- **There is no single "AI in agriculture."** Each region's ecosystem takes a
  different shape, and that shape — not the technology itself — determines
  who controls the data, who captures the value, and what actually gets
  deployed. A vendor-led market, a cooperative data commons, and a
  state-directed programme produce very different outcomes from the same
  underlying tools.
- **The hard questions are structural, not technical.** Whether farmers keep
  control of their data or are locked into vendor platforms; whether
  automation replaces workers or augments them; and whether a farm's
  knowledge survives the hand-over to the next generation — these matter
  more than any single model's accuracy, and they are where the evidence is
  thinnest.

The research's overall stance is neither hype nor dismissal. Independent
verification consistently punctures the vendor story — adoption is thinner
than claimed, headline savings are conditional, and global models fail
outside their training region — while confirming that real, useful gains
exist in the narrow places where deployment is genuine.

## What is actually deployed

The clearest evidence comes from row-crop and grain farming, where the corpus
has independent (non-vendor) data. The pattern there is a **two-speed
structure**:

- **Real deployment** sits in retrofit and sensing cells: camera-based spot
  spraying (which can cut herbicide use substantially, but only under good
  management), grain-cart and tillage autonomy that farmers bolt onto machines
  they already own, and on-combine grain-quality sensing that is now standard
  equipment. These share traits — they fit existing operations, work across
  mixed fleets, and usually carry no per-acre fee.
- **Announcement dominates** the headline cells: full-field autonomy, swarm
  robots, and foundation-model robotics. The largest disclosed autonomous
  deployments run on third-party kits, not the major vendor's own; the
  best-funded robotics companies have no named agricultural deployments at
  all.

Three independent checks keep this honest. First, farm-level adoption is
thin: surveys find only a minority of farms using AI, and mostly for
office and marketing tasks rather than in-field agronomy. (A widely cited
federal decline in precision-agriculture use turns out to be partly a
change in how the survey was run, not a clean drop in adoption — a reminder
to check the measurement before reading the trend.) Second, the headline
"~50% herbicide savings" figure is real but is an average over a
management-sensitive range: the same dial that maximises savings can let
weeds escape and set seed, building a seed bank that erodes the savings over
years. Third, geospatial foundation models are confidently wrong outside
the region and growing season they were trained on — fixable with local
alignment and uncertainty checks, but not ready to map the world out of the
box.

Meanwhile, generative AI has landed first in **reasoning and commerce** —
grain marketing, finance, and advice — not in-field agronomy. That is exactly
where the independent surveys say farmers actually use AI.

## There is no single "AI in agriculture": the ecosystem takes different shapes

The corpus's regional research keeps returning to one finding: countries
facing similar pressures — ageing farmers, rural labour decline, food-security
anxiety — operationalise AI differently, and the difference is structural.
The main shapes:

- **Vendor-led (United States, and wherever the global majors sell).**
  Industrial-machinery companies deploy AI on proprietary platforms. Scale is
  real, but the data and the value flow to the platform owner, and repair and
  data access are tied to subscriptions.
- **Cooperative data commons (Netherlands).** A non-profit cooperative
  infrastructure (JoinData) lets data and benefits flow back to the farmer.
  It depends on a cooperative tradition and cannot simply be transplanted.
- **State-directed (China).** AI arrives inside existing state instruments
  rather than as a standalone strategy, and the state instrumented what it
  owns first — grain reserves, state farms, its own data estate — before it
  instrumented what farmers do. Provincial variation is the implementation
  unit; a closed commercial platform layer sits underneath.
- **Instrument-dispersed (European Union).** The EU has many instruments but
  no agrifood-AI programme, no agrifood seat in AI governance, and regulation
  that reaches machinery rather than farming intelligence. The EU and China
  are near-mirror images: **the EU has instruments without a programme; China
  has a programme without a standalone instrument.** Neither has an "agrifood
  AI policy" in the sense audiences assume.
- **Digital-public-infrastructure substrate (India).** The state builds the
  farmer, plot, and crop registries; private vendors build on top.
- **Industrial automation (Japan) and state-anchored programme (Korea).**
  Japan's machinery majors apply their industrial competence to agriculture
  under a labour-shortage imperative, with a state-stewarded data platform
  (WAGRI). Korea runs an explicit state cluster programme with a quantitative
  adoption target (30% by 2027) and labour substitution as the stated rationale.
- **Other shapes** include Israel's venture-funded startup cluster, the
  Gulf's state-as-standards-setter-and-deployer dual mode, Morocco's
  state-corporate hybrid, Latin America's multilateral-institutional convening,
  and Spain's cooperative-federation substrate.

The point is not a taxonomy for its own sake. The shape of the cluster
decides the things that matter: **who controls the data, who captures the
value, and what counts as a real deployment.**

## Patterns that repeat across regions

Beneath the regional differences, a few structural patterns recur:

- **Clusters are layered mixes, not single patterns.** Most regions run
  several of these shapes at once.
- **A three-structure pattern repeats** in Brazilian seed AI and Spanish
  cooperative AI: a peer-reviewed-research-led tier, a structurally distinct
  middle tier (multinational corporate pipelines in Brazil; the Mondragón
  cooperative federation in Spain), and a thin tier of actual
  deployment-of-record. The research is often ahead of the deployment.
- **State-driven is not the same as state-strategy.** Argentina's
  state-substrate beef traceability differs from the UAE's state dual-mode;
  conflating them hides how the state actually participates.
- **State-led labour substitution has more than one mechanism.** Korea uses a
  statutory adoption target; China substitutes labour through land
  consolidation and its state estates; Japan's is vendor-mediated industrial
  automation. One label does not cover all three.

## The questions that matter

- **Data sovereignty and lock-in.** Farmers distrust digital-tech providers
  with their data — prairie surveys put it near 88–89%. The right-to-repair
  fight makes it concrete: repair access can require a subscription that
  grants the vendor a lasting licence to the machine's data. The cooperative
  commons is the live alternative, and it is culturally substrate-dependent.
- **Labour: substitution or augmentation.** The observed story is mostly
  acreage expansion and automating unglamorous tasks, not replacing workers;
  at realistic labour prices, autonomy often does not beat hired labour. Some
  high-profile consumer-AI deployments were discontinued — a useful negative
  result. State-led substitution is real but mechanism-specific.
- **The farm's knowledge at transfer.** Succession is the missing
  data-continuity layer: when a farm changes hands, the data, knowledge, and
  advisory relationships that AI runs on can be lost. The succession research
  frames this as a substrate and licensing problem, not a tooling problem —
  the transfer moment is where AI-ready continuity is won or lost.
- **The Global South path.** In Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of the Global
  South, multilateral convening, foundation funding, and African-led
  open-source deployment are load-bearing at the deployment layer — a
  different route from the vendor-led North. "Commons by mandate" (China) is a
  different object from "commons by community" (the open-source movement).

## What we can and cannot say

The corpus is disciplined about verification, and the honest limits are part
of the finding:

- **Most deployment-scale figures are vendor-reported and unaudited.** The
  independent evidence is thin; where it exists, it qualifies the vendor
  story rather than confirming it outright.
- **Adoption is thin and office-side**, and the federal trend data carries a
  measurement caveat.
- **Headline savings are conditional** on management, and the seed-bank cost
  of maximum-savings settings is documented in one location, not yet
  replicated across states and commercial machines.
- **Foundation models are region-bound** until locally aligned; the
  mitigations are established for one model in a retrospective study, not yet
  validated in production pipelines.
- **Many gaps are disclosure absences, not research questions** — vendor
  deployment counts, subscription economics by farm size, and challenger
  savings figures simply are not published. More literature cannot close
  them; vendor disclosure or independent audit can.

This is ongoing inquiry, not a settled report. Each synthesis carries its own
open questions and the findings that would change its reading; the gap and
contested-claim registries (corpus-internal, on GitHub) track them.
