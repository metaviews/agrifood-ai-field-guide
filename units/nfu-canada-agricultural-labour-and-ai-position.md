## Content

The National Farmers Union (NFU) provides a documented Canadian producer-side position that treats agricultural labour, automation, farm data, and food sovereignty as connected questions rather than separate policy files. The position is especially useful for the AI-and-labour archetype because it rejects a simple equation of labour shortage with maximum automation.

The primary source is the NFU's October 19, 2022 submission to the federal consultation on a National Agricultural Labour Strategy. A second primary source is the NFU's August 26, 2025 pre-budget consultation submission.

### Automation is not a sufficient labour strategy

The 2022 labour-strategy submission states that the NFU rejects reducing labour costs by maximising the use of technology — specifically naming robots, automation, and self-driving tractors — together with a just-in-time approach to Temporary Foreign Workers. It argues instead for an agricultural labour strategy that recognises the full humanity of farmers and farm workers and the dignity of agricultural work.

This is not a rejection of technology in every context. It is a rejection of treating automation as the default answer to a labour crisis whose causes include farm-income pressure, low returns to labour, rural depopulation, precarious work, and restricted worker mobility.

### Labour shortage as a political-economic problem

The NFU's analysis links farm loss and labour shortage to a cost-price squeeze. Low returns make it difficult for farm operators to pay competitive wages, retain skilled workers, or support new entrants. The result is a cycle involving farm consolidation, rural depopulation, longer hours, and loss of knowledge.

That framing matters for AI analysis: a technology deployment may address a task or reduce a cost while leaving the underlying distribution of value unchanged. The NFU's position therefore supplies a producer-side counterweight to vendor narratives that present automation primarily as a response to a dwindling labour force.

### Rights, migration, and worker voice

The NFU's 2022 submission calls for labour-law protections and the right to unionise for all farm workers, including workers currently excluded from protections in some provinces. It also calls for permanent-residency access for Temporary Foreign Agricultural Workers and improved rights and mobility.

The 2025 submission similarly argues that Canada's agricultural labour strategy cannot be solved by automation and digital technology alone. It calls for full labour rights, open work permits, and a pathway to citizenship for migrant workers, alongside revisions to Temporary Migrant Worker programmes and immigration law.

This provides a primary-source institutional anchor for the labour-and-migration layer, while leaving the specific interaction between named AI deployments and SAWP/TFWP workers as an open empirical gap.

### Data, food sovereignty, and public interest

The NFU's broader policy position places agricultural technology within food sovereignty: the ability of people and nations to define their own food systems, prioritising local production, sustainability, and community well-being. Its farm-data work and policy submissions provide the producer-side institutional context for questions about who controls agricultural data, who benefits from its aggregation, and whether AI strengthens or weakens farmer autonomy.

The 2025 submission also calls for a publicly funded, public-interest agricultural research and extension institution in which farmers, scientists, and agronomists identify problems and share knowledge. That is a useful alternative to a purely vendor-mediated model of agricultural intelligence.

### What this unit closes — and what it does not

This unit closes the primary-source verification gap for the NFU's institutional position on agricultural labour, automation, migrant-worker rights, and food sovereignty (G-386). It strengthens the Canadian labour-and-producer evidence base and gives archetype 08 a directly citable producer-side institutional voice.

It does not establish:

- a specific NFU position on every AI deployment;
- a documented NFU response to the June 2026 AI for All strategy;
- the effect of any named AI deployment on SAWP or TFWP worker numbers;
- workforce-impact or productivity outcomes from automation.

Those remain separate questions.

## Why it matters for talks

- It gives Archetype 08 a Canadian producer-side institutional voice rather than relying only on vendor, academic, or union-side evidence.
- It distinguishes automation as a task intervention from automation as a labour-policy solution.
- It links labour conditions, farm income, migrant-worker rights, succession, data governance, and food sovereignty.
- It supports a Canadian reading in which the question is not simply whether AI reduces labour demand, but whose conditions and bargaining position change.

## Critical context

- The NFU sources are institutional policy submissions and represent the NFU's position; they are not neutral empirical evaluations of every automation deployment.
- The 2022 submission predates the current wave of generative AI and several deployments in the corpus; it is a framework and policy-position anchor, not a current adoption-rate survey.
- The unit does not infer that the NFU opposes all agricultural technology. Its documented objection is to maximising technology as a substitute for addressing labour rights, farm income, and structural conditions.
- SAWP/TFWP and AI deployment interaction remains a substantive gap.
