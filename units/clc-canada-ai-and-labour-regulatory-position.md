## Content

The Canadian Labour Congress (CLC) provides the national labour-centre layer of Canada's AI-and-labour evidence base. Its November 2023 submission to the House of Commons Standing Committee on Industry and Technology on Bill C-27 Part III, the Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA), is a primary-source institutional position on how AI changes work and how federal regulation should respond.

The CLC represents more than 3 million workers through more than 50 national and international unions, provincial and territorial federations of labour, and local labour councils. In 2022 it formed a Task Force on Automation and Artificial Intelligence to study employment, work reorganisation, job design, inequality, and human and labour rights.

### AI changes the organisation of work

The CLC frames AI as a workplace technology, not only a consumer or productivity technology. Its submission identifies effects on:

- employment and job security;
- job design and task composition;
- work organisation;
- workplace monitoring;
- data extraction and management;
- staffing, management, and human-resource processes.

This maps directly onto the field guide's labour distinction between displacement, monitoring, and augmentation. It also provides a national labour-centre basis for reading agrifood computer vision, digital twins, predictive maintenance, scheduling systems, and automated decision systems as workplace-governance questions.

### Outcomes depend on the balance of interests

The CLC's position is conditional rather than technologically deterministic. AI may improve working lives when developed with transparency, accountability, and appropriate intentions. When deployed to shed workers, reduce costs, and pursue profit at the cost of privacy and human rights, it can deepen inequality, insecurity, and discrimination.

The key analytical move is that the same technical capability does not determine a single labour outcome. Governance, bargaining power, transparency, and institutional oversight shape whether AI augments work or intensifies insecurity.

### First principles: transparency and public consultation

The CLC states that transparency and public consultation are indispensable elements of adequate AI regulation. It criticises AIDA's introduction without consultation with unions and civil-society organisations and argues that AI regulation requires a human-rights and labour-rights foundation.

The CLC also argues that AI systems should be subject to human-rights and privacy impact assessment. Its concerns include discrimination, privacy, civil liberties, and the deepening of inequities affecting vulnerable groups.

### The regulatory design the CLC requested

The 2023 submission recommends that:

- AIDA apply to all federal departments, agencies, and Crown corporations, including security institutions;
- the Act be reconceived from a human, labour, and privacy-rights perspective;
- AI risks outside a narrow high-impact classification also be regulated;
- the purpose clause cover harm to individuals and groups, including societal harms;
- the AI and Data Commissioner be independent rather than housed within ISED;
- a permanent, representative AI advisory council include unions, civil society, human-rights advocates, and the public;
- transparency, disclosure, consultation, legislative scrutiny, and oversight be institutionalised rather than left to voluntary industry codes.

The CLC's institutional position is therefore not simply "regulate AI." It specifies who should be inside governance, which rights should be protected, what institutional independence means, and why voluntary codes are inadequate.

### Relevance to AI for All

The CLC's June 4, 2026 response to Canada's AI for All strategy states that Canada's unions are calling for stronger AI laws, independent oversight, protections against surveillance and discrimination, and a greater role for unions in shaping AI use. This makes the CLC a current labour-side counterpoint to an AI strategy centred on adoption, productivity, trust, opportunity, and sovereignty.

The CLC's 2023 AIDA submission and 2026 AI for All statement should be read together: the first supplies the detailed regulatory architecture; the second establishes that the labour-centre's concerns remain active as Canada moves from a proposed AI Act toward an adoption-led national strategy.

### What this unit closes — and what it does not

This unit closes G-387 as a primary-source verification gap for the CLC's AI and labour position and provides the detailed source for G-391 and G-392's regulatory questions.

It does not establish:

- that AIDA became law — Bill C-27 died on January 6, 2025;
- that Canada currently has a federal algorithmic-management statute;
- a specific CLC response to every agriculture measure in AI for All;
- the contents of collective agreements or workplace AI deployments in agrifood;
- quantified workforce effects from a named agrifood AI system.

- It completes the Canadian labour institutional triangle: UFCW Canada (food-chain union), NFU Canada (producer-side position), and CLC (national labour centre).
- It provides primary-source grounding for the claim that Canadian AI governance has been adoption-led while labour-side safeguards remain incomplete.
- It makes algorithmic management, monitoring, transparency, impact assessment, independent oversight, and union participation explicit.
- It gives Archetype 08 a Canadian regulatory frame without claiming that AIDA is current law.

## Critical context

- The detailed source is a November 2023 submission concerning proposed legislation that later died; it is a historical institutional position, not a current statute.
- The June 2026 AI for All statement is current but brief; it does not amount to a sector-specific agriculture policy.
- The unit does not infer that all agrifood AI systems cause the harms identified by the CLC. It supplies the rights-based governance lens for evaluating those risks.
