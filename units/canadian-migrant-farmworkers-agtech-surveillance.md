## Content

Doggett, Ratto, and Chandra's 2024 CHI study, *Migrant Farmworkers' Experiences of Agricultural Technologies: Implications for Worker Sociality and Desired Change*, is the strongest Canada-specific primary research anchor for the migrant-worker and agtech-surveillance gap.

The mixed-methods study is situated in Ontario and combines survey data from 108 migrant farmworkers with individual and group interviews involving 22 workers. It examines how agricultural technologies affect workers' attitudes, conditions, expectations, and visions for supportive change.

### What workers reported

The study focuses particularly on surveillance and tracking technologies, including systems workers referred to as chequeadoras. Its findings identify three connected effects:

1. performance expectations create stress and pressure;
2. surveillance creates fear of disciplinary action;
3. performance tracking heightens competition between workers.

The authors argue that these effects can erode workers' ability to build sociality and solidarity. This is important because the technology is not only measuring work; it is changing the social conditions under which workers can organise, support one another, and contest workplace decisions.

### Automation and the migrant-worker contradiction

The study sits inside a Canadian policy contradiction. Public and industry narratives often frame automation as a way to reduce reliance on temporary or precarious labour, while Ontario greenhouse production continues to depend heavily on migrant workers. The research does not resolve the sector-wide automation question, but it shows why deployment scale alone cannot tell us whether workers benefit.

The relevant question is not only whether a system removes a task. It is also whether the remaining workers experience intensified pace, expanded surveillance, greater competition, reduced solidarity, or new opportunities for skill development.

### Desired change

Workers in the study expressed interest in safer and more ergonomic working environments and opportunities to develop technical skills. The authors also report that workers did not expect employers or government to guarantee these changes without external pressure.

This gives the labour-and-migration layer a worker-defined positive agenda: technology should support safer work, skill development, and collective wellbeing rather than only productivity and control.

### Relationship to the Canadian migrant-worker rights organisations

This unit supplies empirical workplace evidence that complements the organising and rights infrastructure of:

- Migrant Workers Alliance for Change, which provides farmworker rights support, organising, and multilingual information;
- Justicia for Migrant Workers / Harvesting Freedom, which organises around migrant-worker rights, closed permits, workplace safety, and permanent status;
- Migrant Rights Network, a migrant-led coalition addressing status, wage theft, housing, and abuse.

The study is not a statement by these organisations. It is a peer-reviewed research anchor that makes the worker-experience dimension concrete.

### What this unit closes — and what it does not

This unit substantially closes G-394's primary-source evidence gap for migrant-worker experience of agricultural technology and workplace surveillance in Canada. It does not establish:

- the specific vendor or technical architecture of every chequeadora system;
- a national estimate of workers affected by surveillance agtech;
- the effects of a named AI deployment on SAWP or TFWP worker numbers;
- a formal policy position from MWAC, J4MW, or MRN on AI deployment;
- that all Ontario greenhouses use the same systems.

Those remain distinct gaps.

## What this unit is doing in the taxonomy

Anchors the **Canada × migrant agricultural labour × workplace monitoring and agtech** cell. It is the empirical worker-experience complement to the institutional positions of UFCW Canada, NFU Canada, and the CLC.

## Why it matters for talks

- It makes labour monitoring a documented worker-experience issue rather than a hypothetical dual-use concern.
- It connects performance tracking to stress, discipline, competition, and weakened solidarity.
- It prevents the automation story from treating migrant workers only as labour supply or displaced workers.
- It supplies a Canadian case for the Archetype 08 distinction between displacement, monitoring, safety, and worker-defined augmentation.

## Critical context

- The study is Ontario-specific and based on a mixed-methods sample; it should not be generalized to all Canadian farmworkers without qualification.
- The systems are described through worker experience and study terminology; the paper does not provide a full vendor inventory.
- The study is published in CHI 2024 and is peer-reviewed, but its conclusions concern worker experience and desired change rather than national deployment prevalence.
- The organisations named above are complementary institutional context, not co-authors or sources for the study's findings.
